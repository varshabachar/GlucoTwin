SayIndia has an estimated 101 million people living with diabetes, and 136 million with prediabetes — from the ICMR-INDIAB national survey, 2023. A hundred and thirty-six million people who are not diabetic yet, and for most of them, whether they become diabetic is still modifiable. In prediabetes the early warning sign is the post-meal glucose spike. Repeated often enough, that pattern is what progresses toward type 2 diabetes.
The gap

A CGM tells you about a spike.
It just tells you too late.

  • A doctor sees the patient twice a year, and gets one HbA1c — an average over three months.
  • A continuous glucose monitor reports the spike once it is already happening. By then they have already eaten.
  • A simple CGM trend alarm does warn early — at 6.8 false alarms per day. Nobody keeps listening to that.

Can we warn two hours ahead — and say what to do about it?

SayHere's the gap. A doctor sees that patient maybe twice a year. And a CGM will tell you about a spike, but only once it's already happening. So the question I set out to answer is: can you warn someone two hours before the spike, early enough to do something about it? And the second question, which turned out to matter more: can you tell them what to do about it, specifically, for their body?
The system

Static record + live sensors → a twin per patient

SayA digital twin here means two things fused. The static stream — the health record, who the patient is. And the dynamic streams — a Dexcom G6 CGM every five minutes, an Empatica E4 wristband for heart rate and HRV, and a meal log. What the patient is doing right now. Those fuse onto a single five-minute grid. The health record is ingested as a FHIR R4 bundle — LOINC, SNOMED, RxNorm — because that's the standard India's ABDM is built on. Then two things in parallel: a per-patient physiological twin, and a LightGBM fusion model. On the right is what a doctor sees. A classifier tells you that something will happen. A twin lets you ask what if.

The unglamorous part

Three devices. Three clocks.

  • Wristband vs CGM. Heart rate and glucose are both quiet at night. The shift that lines up the two 24-hour patterns is the offset. Here: 0 hours — they agreed.
  • Meal log vs CGM. Glucose should rise 40–80 min after carbs. Participant 016 was logging meals 105 minutes late — corrected.
  • Coverage. Participant 015's entire food log falls outside their CGM recording → marked unavailable, meaning "unknown", not "never ate".

Tested on a deliberately broken copy: every offset recovered exactly, AUROC back to within 0.01.

SayTwo minutes on something that isn't in most projects, because it nearly broke mine. Three devices, each keeping its own clock. If those streams are misaligned and you don't check, the model quietly learns nothing and you never find out. So I check explicitly and print the report. Tell a model someone ate zero carbs when you simply don't know, and you've taught it something false.
Feature design

A feature at time T may only use data from time T or earlier

  • ~60 features — CGM dynamics, wearables, sleep, meals, and 17 from the health record.
  • Person-relative wearables. Resting heart rate varies more between people than within one. Raw values teach a model who someone is — which transfers to a new patient as nothing.
  • Right-closed bins. A bin labelled 12:05 holds data up to and including 12:05, so a prediction "at 12:05" has never seen 12:06.
  • Every rolling statistic is past-only — even the resting heart rate behind the activity estimate.

This is the difference between a glucose model that works and one that is cheating without its author realising.

SayAbout sixty features. Every wearable feature is expressed relative to that person's own normal — their trailing three days — not raw values. Relative features ask the right question: is this person unusual for them, right now? And the single most important rule in this codebase: a feature at time T may only use data from time T or earlier. Only the label looks forward. It's enforced right down to the binning.
The twin

Four numbers are the patient

  • k — carb sensitivity: mg/dL per gram of net carbs
  • tp — minutes to peak after eating
  • a — how much activity helps them
  • tr — how fast they recover

glucose = baseline
  + k × carbs, peaking at tp
  − a × recent movement
  + leftover, fading over tr

Starts at the population average, then re-fitted every day on that patient's own past data only. Only its forecasts feed the ML model — the four parameters are constant per person and would act as an ID card.

SayFor each patient, glucose is modelled as four things added together. Those four numbers are the twin. Everyone starts at the population average — 1.0 mg/dL per gram, peaking at forty-five minutes — then it's re-fitted every day on that patient's own past data only. One deliberate restriction: only the twin's time-varying forecasts go into the machine learning model. The four personal parameters do not — they're constant within a person, so to the model they'd work as an ID card.
Validation

Every number comes from a model that never saw that patient

  • Leave-one-subject-out. Train on 15, test on the 16th, repeat 16 times.
  • Random row splits put the same person in train and test and inflate every score. Don't.
  • Alert thresholds chosen by inner cross-validation on the training patients only — nothing about the test patient leaks into the model or the alarm.
SayHow it's measured matters as much as what it is. Leave-one-subject-out: train on fifteen people, test on the sixteenth, repeat sixteen times. So every number you're about to see comes from a model that has never seen the patient it's scoring. That's the honest way to evaluate a medical model. The alert thresholds are also chosen on the training patients only.

Results

Fusion helps. Wearables alone don't — and I'll say why.

Adding wearables drops the score because the accelerometer files (~13 GB) weren't downloaded — activity is estimated from heart rate, which also rises with stress and after meals. They still contribute inside the full fusion.

SayTwo honest readings. Wearables going down: in this build the accelerometer files weren't downloaded — about thirteen gigabytes — so activity is estimated from heart rate, which is a noisy proxy. And the AUPRC column: random guessing scores 0.22 here, full fusion gets 0.478, a bit over twice baseline. Also note adding the twin's forecasts didn't improve the score — 0.725 against 0.728, well inside the noise for sixteen people. New in this build

A twin that learns from every meal

  • The daily twin re-fits once a day. But a patient doesn't live in days — they live in meals.
  • Before each meal: predict the peak, from parameters fitted on that person's earlier meals only.
  • After the meal: compare with the real CGM peak.
  • Then re-fit, so the next meal is predicted by a slightly better twin.

No leakage, and it is tested, not asserted: a script deletes future meals and future glucose and checks neither the forecast nor the fitted parameters move. 192 + 53 probes, all unchanged.

SayThis is the part I'm most pleased with, and it's new. Before each meal it predicts the peak using parameters fitted on that person's earlier meals only. After the meal, the CGM records what really happened. Then it re-fits. And the claim all of this rests on is that the twin never sees the future — so I don't just assert it. There's a script that deletes future meals and future glucose and checks that neither the forecast nor the fitted parameters move.

SayOver 431 real meals from 15 participants, a generic population twin gets the peak wrong by 32.5 mg/dL. The twin that learns each meal: 25.2. A 22% reduction, bootstrap confidence interval 4.1 to 10.6, so it isn't chance, and it helped 13 of the 15 patients. Over the later half of each person's meals the generic twin drifts to 35.2 while the learning twin holds at 24.8. Now a negative result, which I'm reporting because it's true: I also let the twin use fibre, fat and protein. The improvement was 0.12 mg/dL with a confidence interval that includes zero. It did not measurably help — fibre is barely logged by several participants, and fifteen people is too few for two extra parameters.

Not learned from this data

Eating order is a published constant, and the UI says so

  • The BIG IDEAs food logs record what and when — never in what order. There is nothing here to learn it from.
  • Shukla AP et al., Diabetes Care 2015;38(7):e98–e99 — crossover, n = 11 with type 2 diabetes, reporting 29–37% lower glucose when vegetables and protein came first.
  • I apply 25% — deliberately below their figure, because eleven people eating a Western meal is not this cohort.
  • One named constant: config.ORDER_PEAK_MULTIPLIER. Change it or switch it off.
SayI want to be straight about this. The order effect is not learned from this data — these food logs record what and when, never in what order. It comes from a published crossover trial of eleven patients with type 2 diabetes. They reported 29 to 37 percent lower glucose; I apply 25 percent, deliberately below their figure. It's one named constant in config.py, and the dashboard labels it on screen every time. If order were logged, the twin would personalise this the way it personalises carb sensitivity.
Women's-health module

Insulin sensitivity falls in the luteal phase — and this cohort can't show it

The BIG IDEAs study enrolled only post-menopausal females. All 9 women here are post-menopausal. None has a cycle.

So it ships as a labelled capability demo, behind that warning, defaulting to "Not applicable".

135 → 141

vegetables-first, follicular → luteal. The same advice stops working.

mcPHASES (PhysioNet, 42 women, ~3 months, daily hormone assays) is the dataset that would let the twin learn this.

SayOne more module, with a caveat I want to say out loud. Insulin sensitivity falls in the luteal phase, so the same meal peaks higher. But read the warning — this study enrolled only post-menopausal females. Every woman in this dataset is post-menopausal; none of them has a cycle. So this control isn't telling you anything about this patient. It's a capability demonstration. To actually learn it you'd need mcPHASES — forty-two women, three months each, daily hormone assays. That's in my future work.

Limitations

Arguing against my own project

  • Sixteen people. Feasibility, not clinical validity.
  • The health record is synthetic apart from sex and HbA1c — so the EHR result demonstrates the pipeline, not real records.
  • Activity and sleep are estimated from heart rate, not accelerometry or polysomnography.
  • Unlogged meals can't be anticipated.
  • Eating order and cycle phase are published constants, labelled on screen every time.
  • Food composition didn't help — reported as the negative result it is.
SayLet me close by arguing against my own project, because I'd rather you hear these from me. Sixteen people shows feasibility, not clinical validity. The health record is synthetic apart from sex and HbA1c. Activity and sleep are estimated. Unlogged meals can't be anticipated. The eating order and cycle effects are published constants, not learned here — and the dashboard says so on screen every time. And food composition didn't help, which I've reported as the negative result it is.
